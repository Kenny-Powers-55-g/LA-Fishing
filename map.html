        How scores work

        Each score from 0 to 100 says how good fishing should be for one kind of fish at one spot right now. It's worked out on your phone from live readings, the same way every time.

        How it's worked out

        1. Readings. Every hour OceanCast collects water temperature, tide, wind, pressure, clarity, salinity, rain, river levels and more for each spot.
        2. Each reading gets its own grade for the fish you picked. Redfish do best in water between about 68 and 80°F, for example, so 74°F grades high, 62°F grades lower and 50°F grades low. An ordinary day (no front, no rain, steady water) earns a middling grade; full marks need conditions close to ideal.
        3. Grades are weighted. What matters most for that fish counts more: for redfish, water temperature and tide movement count several times as much as moon phase.
        4. The weighted grades are combined into the score, so one really bad reading (water far too cold, a dead slack tide, rough seas) pulls the score down instead of being averaged away. 75 and up is Excellent, 55 to 74 Good, 35 to 54 Fair, under 35 Poor.

        "What's driving the score" shows the readings that count most. "Why this score" lists every one, with its weight and where it came from.

        Data quality

        Solid data means at least three quarters of the readings this fish needs are available; Partial means half or more; Low means less. The age is how old the readings are. When some are missing, the score uses only the ones it has, so treat Partial and Low scores with more caution.

        If you log catches, OceanCast can adjust the weights to match your own good days. That stays on your device unless you sign in, and you can switch it off under My catches.

        Community tuning. When you log a trip you can also share it anonymously. Once a fish has at least 20 good and 10 slow or empty trips from everyone, readings that graded higher on the good trips count a little more, and ones that graded higher on the slow trips a little less: never more than 30% either way, and less while there are few reports. A report names only one of seven big areas, never a spot, and isn't tied to anyone. Scores that use it say “Community-tuned”, and you can switch it off under My catches.

        Where the data comes from

        • NOAA tide and current stations: water level, water temperature, tidal currents and tide predictions
        • USGS water monitoring stations: water temperature, salinity and water levels in the marsh and rivers
        • National Weather Service: wind and wave forecasts, marine forecasts, weather alerts, river forecasts and radar
        • NOAA buoys (NDBC): offshore wind, waves and sea temperature
        • Open-Meteo: wind, clouds, pressure, rain and the 7-day marine forecast
        • NASA and NOAA satellites: sea temperature, water color and clarity, sea height (Loop Current) and sargassum
        • National Hurricane Center: tropical storms and outlooks
        • BOEM and NOAA: offshore platforms and artificial reefs
        • OpenStreetMap contributors: boat launches (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL)
        • Louisiana Department of Wildlife & Fisheries: size and bag limits and seasons

        Changes to the scoring

        Last changed November 24, 2026: closed seasons for amberjack, red snapper and gray triggerfish; time of year now counts for every fish (so cobia, tripletail, king mackerel and mahi score low once most have left); personal tuning learns each fish separately; distant gauges and buoys are no longer used as if they were at the spot.

        November 21, 2026: tighter ranges for every fish, so "Excellent" means conditions really are close to ideal, and one bad reading now pulls a score down.

        Scores are estimates, not guarantees, and the readings can be hours old or miles from your spot. Always check the official marine forecast before you go out.
